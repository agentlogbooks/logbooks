A worked example, explained from scratch

How do you prove a pile of half-finished work is actually complete?

We built a small multi-step workflow whose database is its to-do list, ran 12 records through four phases, and then asked one question: can you look at the database at any instant and say, for every record, exactly where it stands — with no guessing? This page explains the idea, the test, and the result, assuming you know nothing going in.

The core idea

A to-do list made of empty boxes

Imagine a spreadsheet where each row is a job and each column is a step. The rule is simple: an empty box is a signal that someone should go do that step. A worker scans for empty boxes, does the work, fills the box in, and moves on. The spreadsheet doesn't just record the work — it drives it. There's no separate task manager and nobody assigning jobs by hand.

Plain language

It's like the order rail in a kitchen. A ticket goes up; whoever's free grabs it; when it's done it comes down. Nobody emails the cooks. The board itself is the system. We'll call this kind of database a driver (because it drives the work). The opposite — a tool like a ticketing system where a person assigns each task, sets due dates, and gets reminders — is a tracker. This story is about a driver.

The catch: this only works if an empty box has one unambiguous meaning. Most of this page is about the surprisingly easy ways that breaks — and what it takes to make it trustworthy.

What we built

A four-phase pipeline that accumulates data

Our example enriches a list of companies. Each record starts as just a web domain and gathers more information at every phase. By the end, a sparse row has become a full profile — that's the "data accumulation."

Why these phases matter

Two of them can succeed and still produce nothing: a company might run no detectable software ("none found"), or have no reachable contact ("no contact"). That's a real, finished answer — not a gap. Holding onto that distinction is the whole game, as you'll see.

What "data complete" really means

The empty-box trap

Here's the problem in one picture. Two of our records look byte-for-byte identical in their data, yet they mean opposite things:

umbrella.com

Truth: COMPLETE

We checked. This company runs no detectable software. Empty is the correct, final answer.

cyberdyne.com

tech-stack columns: empty

Truth: GAVE UP

Every lookup errored out. Empty means nobody ever finished this step. Opposite meaning, same bytes.

If your only signal is "is the content box empty?", these two are indistinguishable — and so is a third case: a record someone is actively working on right now looks finished the moment its last box is filled, even though it isn't released yet.

The takeaway

The fix isn't a cleverer query — no query over the naive data can separate identical rows. The fix is storing a tiny status flag for each phase whose only job is to say pending / done / finished-empty / gave-up. That one column per phase is what turns "are we done?" from a guess into a fact.

How we checked it

We didn't just trust our own test

We ran a 9-agent review. Six independent checkers each wrote their own database query from scratch to try to find a broken record. One adversary tried to find a hole the six would miss. One tried to reproduce our clean answer on the naive design. One wrote the verdict.

All six checkers: zero broken records.   Naive design: failed (couldn't get past 7/12).

The one thing we found

A blind spot the snapshot can't see

The adversary found a real gap — not in the data, but in the design. We only count a record's failed attempts when a worker reports an error. But a worker could grab a record, crash before doing anything, and silently let its claim expire. The record gets handed back out — with its attempt count untouched. That can repeat forever, and because no error is ever reported, the "give up after 3 tries" rule never fires. A photo of the database at any instant looks perfectly healthy.

The fix (a general rule, noted for the skill): count an attempt the moment a worker grabs a record, not only when it reports an error — and let the hand-back step itself flag a record that's been grabbed too many times. Then a crash-loop trips the limit like any other failure.

Bottom line

"Is our data complete?" becomes a question with a provable answer

With one small status flag per phase, every record is always accountable — done, finished-empty, flagged, in-flight, or waiting — and you can verify the whole pile with a single query that six independent reviewers couldn't break. Without those flags, the best you can do is guess, and the guess is wrong 5 times out of 12. That's the difference the pattern buys.
